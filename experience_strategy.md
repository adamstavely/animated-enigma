# Unified Portfolio Experience
## Executive Strategy for a Coherent, Connected Application Ecosystem

> **One recognizable portfolio. Clear product boundaries. Connected mission workflows.**

---

## 1. Executive Summary

We will improve the experience of our application portfolio by organizing it around the work users need to accomplish, reducing unnecessary fragmentation, and establishing a coherent product family.

Today, independently developed applications can impose a cumulative burden on users: overlapping capabilities, inconsistent terminology and interfaces, repeated configuration, manual transfers between systems, and disconnected sources of help and information. Each application may work adequately on its own while the overall experience remains difficult.

This strategy addresses that burden through six coordinated workstreams:

1. **Convergence and integration** to remove duplication and connect complementary capabilities.
2. **Feature-versus-application discipline** to prevent unnecessary portfolio growth.
3. **Brand cohesion** to establish a recognizable family of products.
4. **Interface inventory and standardization** to create consistent visual and interaction behavior.
5. **Standardized user portals** to make release notes, roadmaps, feedback, and help discoverable.
6. **In-app guided tutorials** to support learning in the flow of work.

Seven enterprise-level practices strengthen these workstreams: experience architecture, portfolio information architecture, experience integration contracts, quality gates, common personalization, experience intelligence, and product maturity assessment.

Together, these establish more than a common appearance. They define how the portfolio is organized, how products connect, how quality is assessed, and how improvement is sustained.

The intended result is an ecosystem comparable in cohesion to Google’s application family: products have distinct purposes, yet users recognize their relationship, understand familiar interactions, and move between them with confidence.

**Success will be measured by improved mission workflows—not by application count or visual consistency alone.**

## 2. Vision, Mission, and Brand Promise

### Vision

**One coherent portfolio where users know where to work, carry context between capabilities, and build on what they already know.**

### Mission

**Improve mission performance by simplifying the application landscape, connecting workflows, and providing shared experience standards, services, and enablement.**

### Brand promise

**Familiar. Connected. Accessible. Dependable.**

| Promise | What users should experience |
| --- | --- |
| **Familiar** | Common actions, terminology, and interaction patterns behave predictably across products. |
| **Connected** | Relevant work and context move between products without unnecessary re-entry or reconstruction. |
| **Accessible** | Users can complete important tasks across supported abilities, devices, and operating conditions. |
| **Dependable** | Users understand system status, permissions, errors, recovery, and where to obtain help. |

## 3. Strategic Goals and Success Measures

| Goal | Executive intent | Evidence of success |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **G1. Simplify the portfolio** | Give every product a defensible purpose and reduce unnecessary duplication. | Duplicate implementations removed; approved dispositions completed; replaced applications retired. |
| **G2. Connect mission work** | Reduce the effort required to complete workflows across products. | Fewer manual handoffs, less re-entry, fewer context errors, improved completion time. |
| **G3. Establish a familiar experience** | Make learning transferable between products. | Better cross-product task performance; consistent critical patterns; fewer accessibility barriers. |
| **G4. Improve discovery** | Help users understand the portfolio and find the right capability. | Improved navigation and findability; reduced time to locate the correct product or function. |
| **G5. Accelerate proficiency** | Help users complete important tasks independently sooner. | Reduced time to first successful task; improved independent task success; lower support demand. |
| **G6. Make change and feedback transparent** | Give users reliable information and a visible path to influence improvement. | Current portals; timely feedback responses; improved understanding of changes. |
| **G7. Sustain improvement responsibly** | Direct investment using evidence of value, readiness, and lifecycle health. | Improved maturity profiles; verified benefits; managed exceptions; reduced avoidable operating burden. |

### Measurement principles

- Establish baselines before delivering major interventions.
- Evaluate the **complete workflow**, including transitions between products.
- Report results by relevant user population and operating context.
- Include critical and occasional workflows, not only frequently used tasks.
- Pair adoption and conformance measures with task performance.
- Distinguish verified financial savings from avoided cost and estimated user time saved.
- Report regressions and evidence limitations alongside improvements.

> **A lower application count paired with worse mission performance is a failed intervention.**

## 4. Guiding Principles

### 4.1 Workflows first, applications second

The primary unit of analysis is the outcome users need to achieve. Existing application boundaries are inputs to the analysis, not constraints on the answer.

### 4.2 Evidence over ownership and history

Research, usage, task performance, capability mapping, and operating requirements guide portfolio decisions. Ownership, funding history, and sunk cost provide context but do not establish the best user experience.

### 4.3 Extend before creating

The default response to a new need is to configure, extend, compose, integrate, or reuse existing capabilities. A new application must demonstrate why those approaches are insufficient.

### 4.4 Choose the lightest effective intervention

Standardization, integration, federation, convergence, consolidation, and retirement solve different problems. They are options to evaluate—not a mandatory sequence of increasingly large changes.

### 4.5 Shared foundations, purposeful differences

Common actions should use common patterns. Specialized workflows may differ when user research or mission requirements justify the difference.

### 4.6 Better than parity

A replacement must preserve required outcomes and remove measurable friction. Replicating the existing experience inside a larger product is insufficient.

### 4.7 Standards are sustained products

Shared components, navigation, preference services, portals, and learning tools require accountable owners, maintained documentation, support, and funded capacity.

### 4.8 Retirement is part of consolidation

Consolidation is incomplete while replaced applications remain in routine operation. Temporary coexistence requires an owner, expiry, and exit criteria.

### 4.9 Change is part of the experience

Communication, support, onboarding, redirects, and feedback are delivery responsibilities—not activities added after implementation.

### 4.10 Shared experience does not erase mission boundaries

Common identity and navigation do not imply unrestricted data access. Authorization, security markings, records obligations, and operating constraints remain explicit.

## 5. Common Vocabulary

### 5.1 Building blocks

| Term | Definition | Test question | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Capability** | An implementation-independent ability the organization needs. | What must the organization be able to do, regardless of the tool? | Find evidence; manage requirements; disseminate products. |
| **Task** | A discrete action performed by a role toward a sub-goal. | What specific step is the user completing? | Compare two records; approve a request. |
| **Feature** | A bounded function supporting tasks within a broader product or workflow. | Does this function belong within an existing job or experience? | Saved filters; bulk editing; export; a map layer. |
| **Workflow** | A sequence of tasks and decisions producing a defined mission or business outcome, potentially across roles and products. | What triggers the work, and what constitutes completion? | Request, review, approve, and publish a product. |
| **Application** | A user-facing product with a coherent purpose, responsibility for complete workflows, accountable ownership, and a managed lifecycle. | Does it warrant a distinct place in the user’s mental model? | A case-management product or analytic workbench. |
| **Module or microapp** | A bounded experience hosted within a larger product under shared navigation, branding, and experience contracts. | Can it serve its purpose within an existing product? | A comparison tool within an analytic workspace. |
| **SuperApp** | A unified product composing related workflows, modules, and capabilities for a coherent mission domain or population. | Does unifying these experiences improve a meaningful body of work? | A workbench combining related analytic workflows. |
| **Capability platform** | A productized shared implementation of capabilities with defined contracts, ownership, and service expectations. | Should multiple products consume this implementation? | Shared search, notifications, or document services. |

**Important distinctions**

A feature can be independently deployed, permissioned, or deep-linked without becoming a standalone application.

A capability is not inherently an API or a backend service. Its implementation may include services, interfaces, processes, or several of these.

A workflow can cross multiple applications. The goal is coherent work, not an assumption that every workflow must fit inside one product.

### 5.2 Portfolio interventions

| Intervention | Definition | User-facing effect | Effect on application count |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Standardization** | Adopt common visual, content, accessibility, and interaction standards. | Familiar appearance and behavior. | Usually unchanged. |
| **Integration** | Exchange authorized data, context, and actions between distinct products. | Easier handoffs and less manual transfer. | Usually unchanged. |
| **Federation** | Present independently delivered modules within a shared shell. | Unified access and navigation. | Entry points may decline; implementations may remain. |
| **Convergence** | Move toward shared capabilities, contracts, components, and operating patterns. | Greater consistency and less unnecessary duplication. | May remain unchanged. |
| **Consolidation** | Combine functionality into a destination product and retire replaced experiences. | Fewer places to complete related work. | Decreases when retirement completes. |
| **Retirement** | Withdraw an application or implementation after resolving workflow, dependency, and data obligations. | Obsolete or replaced experience is removed. | Decreases. |

**Convergence and consolidation are related, but different.** Shared implementations can support several distinct products. Conversely, a shared shell can conceal duplicate implementations that still need attention.

## 6. Feature-versus-Application Discipline

### 6.1 Placement decision ladder

Every proposal evaluates the following options and stops where a credible solution exists:

| Order | Option | Decision question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1** | Configure | Can an existing product meet the need through configuration or supported settings? |
| **2** | Extend | Can the need become a feature within an existing product? |
| **3** | Compose | Can it become a module within an existing SuperApp? |
| **4** | Integrate | Can existing products meet the need through improved connections? |
| **5** | Reuse or build a shared capability | Does the reusable implementation belong on a capability platform, surfaced through existing experiences? |
| **6** | Create an application | Is a distinct product boundary necessary after credible alternatives are assessed? |

Platform placement and experience placement may both be needed. Teams document alternatives; they do not need to build and fail each one.

### 6.2 Criteria for a new application

| Criterion | Required evidence |
| --- | --- |
| **Coherent unmet job** | Defined users, workflows, outcomes, and evidence of unmet need. |
| **No credible existing home** | Assessment of the closest products, including extension, composition, and integration alternatives. |
| **Durable product boundary** | Explanation of why a standalone experience improves the work or satisfies a material operating requirement. |
| **Lifecycle viability** | Named owner, delivery and support capacity, total-cost estimate, and credible sustainment funding. |
| **Shared-foundation adoption** | Commitment to portfolio branding, design standards, identity/access conventions, portals, and enablement. |
| **Experience readiness** | Research and validation evidence appropriate to the approval stage. |
| **Measurable value** | Success measures, review date, and conditions for consolidation or retirement. |
| **Portfolio approval** | Recorded decision through the designated governance authority. |

### 6.3 Signals that a proposal may be a feature or module

- Its main entry point is another product’s workflow.
- It supports a bounded step rather than a complete job.
- It serves substantially the same users and context as an existing product.
- Its functionality could be discovered naturally within existing navigation.
- Its standalone justification depends primarily on team autonomy, technology choice, or delivery speed.

These are assessment signals, not automatic rules. Shared users or data do not prove products should merge, and infrequent emergency or specialist work may justify a distinct application.

## 7. Criteria for a New SuperApp

A SuperApp is justified when unifying a coherent body of work removes more friction than it introduces.

### 7.1 Entry criteria

| Criterion | Evidence required |
| --- | --- |
| **Coherent audience or mission domain** | Research establishing related outcomes, roles, and navigation needs. |
| **Demonstrated fragmentation** | Workflow evidence showing costly switching, handoffs, or duplicated effort. |
| **Meaningful context benefit** | Entity and task-context mapping showing what can be preserved or shared. |
| **No better existing home** | Comparison with existing SuperApp expansion, integration, and federation. |
| **Platform and delivery readiness** | Available or funded dependencies, supported contracts, and delivery capacity. |
| **Net simplification** | Explicit changes to products, entry points, and duplicate implementations. |
| **Operable boundaries** | Performance, resilience, permissions, records, and disconnected-use needs addressed. |
| **Single experience accountability** | One owner responsible for the complete product, with named module and platform owners. |

### 7.2 Design obligations

An approved SuperApp provides:

- Coherent navigation and module discovery.
- Relevant context across related workflows.
- Consistent search and notification conventions with visible scope.
- Shared help, preference, and account patterns.
- Accessible transitions and recovery.
- Supported module contracts that allow delivery autonomy.
- Product-level release notes, roadmap, feedback, and onboarding.

### 7.3 Warning signs

- Modules share an organization or funding line but have little workflow relationship.
- The proposal is primarily a page of links.
- Navigation becomes a menu of everything.
- A unified interface conceals unresolved context or data conflicts.
- Replaced products have no credible retirement plan.
- Shared dependencies lack ownership or operating capacity.

A bounded pilot may temporarily add an experience before retirement. A genuinely new mission domain may have nothing to retire. Both cases require explicit justification and review.

## 8. Six Core Workstreams

| Workstream | Executive objective | Principal deliverables |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Convergence and integration** | Remove duplication and workflow friction. | Workflow catalog; capability map; seam and overlap analysis; dispositions; delivery waves; retirement plans. |
| **2. Feature-versus-application discipline** | Prevent unnecessary portfolio growth. | Placement policy; application and SuperApp criteria; intake and funding reviews. |
| **3. Brand cohesion** | Establish a recognizable, distinguishable product family. | Brand architecture; naming convention; icon system; shared chrome; content guidance. |
| **4. Interface inventory and standardization** | Establish consistent visual and interaction behavior. | Foundation, component, and pattern inventories; gap register; design-system roadmap. |
| **5. Standardized user portals** | Make changes, plans, feedback, and support trustworthy and discoverable. | Common publishing model; product-filtered views; feedback lifecycle; content ownership. |
| **6. In-app guided tutorials** | Support learning in the flow of work. | Shared tooling; task-based guidance; accessible alternatives; content lifecycle; proficiency measures. |

### 8.1 Convergence and integration

Assess both the workflow and the implementations supporting it.

The portfolio review identifies:

- Where users cross application boundaries.
- Which crossings require manual transfer or reconstruction.
- Where capabilities overlap.
- Which products have defensible, distinct purposes.
- Which implementations should become shared.
- Which experiences should be consolidated or retired.

Each product receives a primary disposition—retain, invest, integrate, federate, converge, consolidate, or retire—with supporting interventions recorded separately.

Prioritization considers user pain, mission criticality, overlap, operating cost, technical health, strategic fit, and delivery feasibility.

### 8.2 Brand cohesion

Adopt a **branded-house direction**: one master identity with products that remain distinguishable by purpose.

| Dimension | Strategic requirement |
| --- | --- |
| **Master brand** | Define the portfolio identity and promise; select the name through an explicit leadership decision. |
| **Product names** | Use a consistent naming relationship and clear descriptions of purpose; retain useful legacy aliases. |
| **Icon family** | Share construction rules while preserving distinct silhouettes and text labels. |
| **Color** | Standardize semantic meanings; control accents; preserve required security markings. |
| **Typography** | Use supported fonts, hierarchy, and readable density across products. |
| **Universal chrome** | Place common functions predictably and use consistent behavior. |
| **Voice and terminology** | Standardize common actions, errors, empty states, and recovery guidance. |
| **Entry and exit moments** | Align sign-in, loading, session expiry, permission denial, and unsaved-work handling. |
| **Motion** | Use restrained, consistent feedback with reduced-motion support. |
| **Accessibility** | Treat accessible behavior as part of the family promise. |
| **Legacy transition** | Coordinate names, redirects, communications, and retirement. |
| **Stewardship** | Assign ownership for approved assets and exceptions. |

The Google-family analogy establishes cohesion, not a requirement to imitate its icons or visual styling.

### 8.3 Interface inventory and standardization

Extend the existing enterprise design system.

| Inventory layer | Coverage |
| --- | --- |
| **Foundations** | Frameworks, semantic colors, iconography, typography, spacing, layout, density, themes, responsiveness, accessibility. |
| **Components** | Navigation, controls, forms, tables, maps, charts, overlays, notifications, loading/error/empty states. |
| **Patterns** | Search, filtering, editing, validation, permissions, context handoff, collaboration, visualization, recovery, and learning. |

Each finding records the affected workflow, evidence, severity, populations, owner, remediation, and exception status.

Prioritize consequential workflow and accessibility problems ahead of cosmetic differences.

### 8.4 Standardized portals

Use one governed publishing and feedback model, surfaced through portfolio-wide and product-specific views.

| Surface | Minimum expectation |
| --- | --- |
| **Release notes** | Explain changes, affected users, benefits, required actions, effective version/date, limitations, and help. |
| **Roadmap** | Distinguish discovery, planned, in-progress, and delivered work; make uncertainty visible. |
| **Feedback** | Acknowledge, route, deduplicate, track, and explain decisions to authorized users. |
| **Help** | Provide searchable task guidance, support routes, known issues, and current version information. |

Define content owners and service expectations. Protect sensitive submissions and provide approved equivalents for disconnected environments.

### 8.5 Guided tutorials

Prioritize top tasks, costly errors, and materially changed workflows.

Guidance should be:

- Contextual, optional, dismissible, and replayable.
- Accessible by keyboard and assistive technology.
- Supported by static instructions and captioned/transcribed media.
- Versioned and maintained alongside product changes.
- Available through approved delivery mechanisms.
- Evaluated through subsequent independent task performance.

## 9. Enterprise Experience Architecture
### High priority · Defines how the ecosystem behaves

Applications can share components and still feel disconnected. Establish an **Enterprise Experience Architecture standard** covering navigation, switching, context preservation, object terminology, notifications, search, and workflow transitions.

| Layer | Experience responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| **Portfolio** | Product discovery, relationships, shared conventions, and cross-product patterns. |
| **SuperApp** | Domain navigation, module composition, workspace context, and complete workflow coherence. |
| **Application** | Task experience, supported entry and return paths, context handling, and recovery. |
| **Module** | Conformance to host navigation, context, focus, permissions, and lifecycle contracts. |
| **Shared platforms** | Reliable supporting services with explicit ownership and failure behavior. |

**Deliverables:** Responsibility map, reference patterns, validated journeys, and boundary/exception register.

**Accountability:** Experience leadership and enterprise architecture jointly steward the standard; product owners remain accountable for their end-to-end journeys.

## 10. Portfolio Information Architecture
### High priority · Defines how users understand and discover the portfolio

Create a shared taxonomy for mission domains, products, modules, workflows, tasks, and common entities.

Apply it across the launcher, navigation, search, help, and cross-product discovery.

The taxonomy includes:

- Canonical names and plain-language descriptions.
- Relationships between products and workflows.
- Consistent object terminology.
- Synonyms and legacy aliases.
- Navigation placement and discovery rules.
- Permission-aware visibility.
- Change management and redirects.

Validate the model through representative findability and navigation testing.

**Deliverables:** Portfolio taxonomy, navigation model, naming glossary, and discovery patterns.

**Outcome:** Users understand where capabilities belong and can locate the right place to work.

## 11. Experience-Level Integration Contracts
### High priority · Defines what a usable handoff requires

Technical APIs are necessary, but insufficient. Every material cross-product workflow requires an experience contract.

| Contract element | Required behavior |
| --- | --- |
| **Entry** | Supported deep links and understandable handling of missing or retired destinations. |
| **Context** | Relevant task, entity, workspace, selection, or filter context transferred within allowed boundaries. |
| **Authorization** | Destination access checks, permitted identity continuity, and clear denial or reauthentication. |
| **Return** | Predictable return paths and preservation of permitted source state. |
| **Work protection** | Explicit handling of unsaved work, expired sessions, and interrupted actions. |
| **Recovery** | Accessible loading, timeout, failure, retry, and support behavior. |
| **Accessibility** | Focus handoff, keyboard paths, meaningful labels, and announcements. |
| **Lifecycle** | Owners, compatibility, change notice, deprecation, and redirects. |
| **Acceptance** | End-to-end handoff, context, completion, return, and error measures. |

Shared identity never overrides authorization. If context cannot transfer, users receive an understandable explanation and an approved alternative.

**Deliverable:** Standard experience integration contract linked to technical contracts.

## 12. Experience Quality Gate
### High priority · Defines when an experience is ready

Introduce readiness reviews at product approval, major release, and migration cutover.

| Review | Required evidence |
| --- | --- |
| **Product approval** | User need, workflow research, placement alternatives, design-system fit, accessibility risks, and validation/support plans. |
| **Major release** | Task usability, accessibility, standards conformance, integration behavior, support, and onboarding readiness. |
| **Migration cutover** | Required-outcome coverage, population-specific task results, data/context integrity, support, and tested rollback. |

Approval-stage evidence is proportionate to maturity: a proposal requires a credible validation plan; a release requires demonstrated results.

No unresolved critical experience blocker passes the gate. Other deviations require an approved exception with an owner, impact, remediation, and expiry.

**Deliverables:** Readiness checklist, acceptance thresholds, evidence record, and decision conditions.

## 13. Common Personalization Framework
### Medium priority · Defines how settings follow the user

Establish consistent preference contracts for accessibility settings, display choices, notifications, saved views, favorites, and frequently used capabilities.

| Setting category | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| **Enterprise defaults and policy** | Establish permitted choices and organizational constraints. |
| **Personal preferences** | Provide reusable user defaults across supported products. |
| **Mission/workspace settings** | Apply context-specific choices where permitted. |
| **Session choices** | Support temporary changes without silently persisting them. |

Define scope, precedence, synchronization, fallback, reset, retention, and ownership. Users should understand where a setting applies.

Domain-specific settings need not synchronize when their meanings differ. Saved views never confer access to underlying data.

**Deliverable:** Common preference framework and supported contracts.

---

## 14. Portfolio Experience Intelligence
### Medium priority · Defines how the portfolio learns

Establish a common measurement practice spanning workflows, usability, adoption, feedback, accessibility, and operational friction.

Combine:

- Product analytics.
- Cross-product journey evidence.
- User research and usability benchmarks.
- Support and feedback trends.
- Accessibility findings.
- Operational failure evidence.

Define consistent task starts, completions, handoffs, errors, retries, and abandonment. Use approved, purpose-limited event standards with appropriate minimization, access, retention, and correlation.

Avoid sensitive task content and unnecessary personal identifiers. Where tracking or connectivity is restricted, use approved aggregate evidence and research methods.

**Deliverables:** Metric dictionary, event standards, journey measurement framework, and portfolio scorecard.

**Outcome:** Leadership can identify failures between products and determine whether interventions improve complete workflows.

## 15. Product Maturity Model
### Medium priority · Defines realistic progress toward the target state

Assess every product across ownership, research, task performance, accessibility, standards adoption, interoperability, onboarding, and operational health.

| Level | Description | Investment emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **M0. Unassessed** | Evidence or ownership is incomplete. | Establish accountability and assess risk. |
| **M1. Accountable** | Purpose, owner, workflows, risks, support, and lifecycle are documented. | Address foundational gaps. |
| **M2. Standardized** | Shared patterns, accessible critical tasks, portals, and guidance are established. | Strengthen adoption and remediate deviations. |
| **M3. Connected** | Relevant integration, context, preferences, and journey measurement are validated. | Remove priority seams and duplication. |
| **M4. Outcome-managed** | Sustained performance is measured and continuously improved. | Scale proven patterns and manage lifecycle value. |

Assess dimensions separately. An average score must not conceal a critical weakness.

Maturity informs modernization and investment; it does not independently determine disposition. A mature duplicate may still be retired, while an immature mission-critical product may require investment.

**Deliverables:** Evidence-based maturity profiles and prioritized improvement roadmaps.

## 16. Governance and Decision Rights

Use existing governance with explicit authority, rather than create parallel committees by default.

| Decision or responsibility | Proposed accountable authority |
| --- | --- |
| Portfolio outcomes, investment priorities, and delegated authority | Executive sponsor |
| New applications, SuperApps, and dispositions | Architecture review board under delegation |
| Enterprise experience architecture and information architecture | Experience/design lead with enterprise architecture |
| Brand, naming, and common experience standards | Experience/design lead with communications and product input |
| Shared components and platform contracts | Design-system and platform owners |
| Product readiness and end-to-end performance | Product owner |
| Portals, learning, and change enablement | User enablement lead with product owners |
| Experience intelligence and maturity reporting | Portfolio experience lead with research and analytics |

### Governance mechanisms

- Embed placement reviews in intake, architecture, and funding decisions.
- Apply readiness gates at approval, major release, and cutover.
- Maintain one decision and exception register.
- Record rationale, evidence, owners, conditions, and review dates.
- Make temporary deviations expire.
- Provide an evidence-based appeal route.
- Retain independent security, records, accessibility, and financial authorities.

Fund shared foundations as sustained products. Include migration, coexistence, support, training, and destination operating costs in wave decisions.

## 17. Delivery Approach

### Stage 1 — Establish the foundation

Confirm authority and ownership; inventory products and workflows; capture baselines; define placement policy, experience architecture, taxonomy, and quality gates.

Begin preference contracts, measurement standards, and maturity assessments.

**Gate:** A bounded pilot has approved scope, owners, measures, dependencies, and capacity.

### Stage 2 — Prove the approach

Deliver a representative workflow improvement. Validate navigation, integration contracts, shared patterns, portals, guided learning, preferences, and measurement.

**Gate:** Demonstrated benefit with no unacceptable critical regression; support and rollback are ready.

### Stage 3 — Deliver coordinated waves

Sequence convergence, integration, migration, brand transitions, enablement, and retirement around affected populations and dependencies.

**Gate:** Cutover and retirement evidence accepted; outcomes and costs reported.

### Stage 4 — Sustain and expand

Review outcomes, maturity, exceptions, product purpose, and shared-service health. Scale interventions justified by evidence.

**Gate:** Subsequent investment follows demonstrated value and available capacity.

The first-wave emphasis is the four high-priority enhancements: experience architecture, information architecture, integration contracts, and quality gates. The three medium-priority enhancements begin during foundation and mature through delivery.

## 18. Key Risks and Responses

| Risk | Response |
| --- | --- |
| Visual cohesion masks disconnected workflows. | Require journey evidence and integration acceptance. |
| SuperApps become overloaded collections of functions. | Validate domain coherence, navigation, and task performance. |
| Rebranding creates unnecessary relearning. | Coordinate naming with migration and retirement. |
| Standards become checkbox compliance. | Pair conformance with usability and accessibility results. |
| Shared services become delivery or operating bottlenecks. | Fund capacity, ownership, service expectations, and recovery. |
| Consolidation loses necessary functionality. | Validate required outcomes by population and test rollback. |
| Temporary coexistence becomes permanent. | Establish expiry, write authority, dependencies, and retirement ownership. |
| Analytics overstate improvement. | Disclose baselines, denominators, coverage, and confidence. |
| Cross-product context exposes sensitive information. | Minimize transferred context and enforce destination authorization. |
| Teams resist portfolio changes. | Engage early, publish evidence, provide meaningful destination roles and an appeal path. |

## 19. Leadership Decisions Required

Leadership should:

1. Ratify the shared vocabulary and placement policy.
2. Confirm sponsor, delegated authority, and accountable owners.
3. Approve the branded-house direction and master-brand selection process.
4. Fund shared experience foundations and a bounded pilot.
5. Approve the quality-gate, exception, and measurement approach.
6. Establish portfolio maturity and lifecycle reviews.
7. Require demonstrated workflow benefits before scaling major interventions.

**The strategic commitment is to a coherent ecosystem that makes mission work easier, with every product and shared capability earning its place through evidence.**
